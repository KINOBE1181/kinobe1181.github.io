Can AI Agents Be Trusted to Operate Without Human Supervision?

Research opinion article · Rowan University · 2026

Artificial intelligence has come a long way in recent years, especially with the development of AI agents that can perform tasks without needing constant instructions. These systems can write code, search for information, use tools, and work through problems that would normally require someone to complete them manually. As someone interested in AI engineering and computer systems, I think this technology is fascinating, and I can see how useful it could become. However, I also believe there needs to be a limit to how much independence we give these systems.

My biggest concern is what happens when an AI agent makes a mistake and nobody is there to catch it. It is one thing for a chatbot to give someone an incorrect answer, but another thing entirely when an AI agent has access to important files, databases, or computer systems. A mistake in those situations could cause problems that are much harder to fix. Research has demonstrated both the capabilities of AI agents and the risks associated with letting them act independently (Yao et al., 2023; Ruan et al., 2024). I believe AI agents have enormous potential, but they should assist people rather than completely replace human judgment.

The Benefits of AI Agents and Why Human Review Matters

Even with my concerns, I still believe AI agents have a lot to offer. In ReAct, Yao et al. (2023) explored how language models can combine reasoning with actions to complete tasks. Instead of simply producing an answer, a model can work through a problem, interact with an outside source, observe the result, and continue toward a solution.

The researchers evaluated ReAct on question-answering, fact-verification, and interactive decision-making tasks. Their results showed that combining reasoning and action could improve performance compared with some alternative approaches (Yao et al., 2023). I find this interesting because it shows how AI systems can become more useful when they can gather information instead of depending entirely on what they already know.

I can imagine this being helpful in software development, research, and troubleshooting. For example, an agent might help a programmer investigate an error, find documentation, and suggest a possible fix. That could save time and let the programmer focus on more complicated work.

However, successfully completing a task does not mean an AI agent should automatically be trusted without supervision. Even when a result looks correct, there could be small mistakes that are easy to overlook. Those mistakes might become larger problems if nobody checks them. I believe human review should remain part of the process. The purpose of AI should be to make people's jobs easier, not remove their responsibility for making sure the work is correct.

The Risks of Giving AI Agents Too Much Control

Of the four studies, the one that concerns me most is ToolEmu. Ruan et al. (2024) developed a framework that simulates how AI agents interact with external tools. This allows researchers to investigate dangerous situations without exposing real systems to the same risks.

The researchers evaluated agents using 144 test cases across 36 high-stakes toolkits. They identified potentially severe failures, including risks involving private information and financial losses. Even the safest agent in their evaluation exhibited potentially serious failures in 23.9% of cases according to an automated evaluator (Ruan et al., 2024). That number describes the study's particular simulated tests, not the failure rate of all AI agents.

What worries me is not artificial intelligence itself, but what could happen when these systems receive too much responsibility without proper supervision. An AI agent could be designed to help and still make a mistake that causes serious harm.

My concerns are mainly about two things: protecting sensitive information and preventing harmful decisions in important systems. If an agent has access to someone's personal or financial information, an incorrect action could expose data that should remain private. An agent working with healthcare, banking, or cybersecurity systems could also make a decision that negatively affects the people relying on those services. I consider both situations equally serious because the consequences could extend far beyond the original task.

ToolEmu makes me question whether developers and organizations always do enough testing before giving agents access to important systems. The study shows why it matters to look deliberately for risky behavior before deployment (Ruan et al., 2024). I believe the people developing and deploying these systems should establish safeguards, limit access to sensitive resources, and require appropriate approval for important actions. Human oversight, restricted permissions, and thorough testing should work together.

AI-Generated Code and Human Responsibility

Another area where I believe oversight is especially important is software engineering. SWE-bench, introduced by Jimenez et al. (2024), examines how well language models can resolve real software issues taken from GitHub repositories.

The researchers created a benchmark containing 2,294 software engineering problems from 12 Python repositories. These tasks required models to understand existing code and propose changes to resolve reported issues. In the original evaluation, the strongest tested model, Claude 2, resolved only 1.96% of the issues (Jimenez et al., 2024). That result reflects the models and evaluation methods used in the original study, not the capabilities of every AI system today.

I find this research important because writing code that looks correct is not the same as understanding how it affects an entire application. Software systems have multiple components that depend on one another. Changing one function could unexpectedly affect another part of the program.

Personally, I believe code generated by an AI agent should be reviewed by someone qualified to understand it. Automated testing is useful, but passing tests does not automatically mean code is completely safe or correct. Tests can miss situations their authors did not anticipate. An AI-generated change might solve the original problem while introducing a security vulnerability or unexpected behavior elsewhere.

My experience studying database transactions and concurrency has made me more aware of how important small implementation details can be. A program might work under normal conditions but behave differently when multiple operations happen at once. AI agents can be valuable assistants for programmers, but qualified developers should inspect important changes, understand their effects, and remain responsible for approving them before deployment.

Cybersecurity Risks and the Importance of Explicit Permission

Another concern I have is cybersecurity, especially when AI agents interact with information from outside sources. AgentDojo, introduced by Debenedetti et al. (2024), investigates prompt-injection attacks: malicious instructions placed in information an agent encounters while completing a legitimate task.

The researchers developed a testing environment containing 97 realistic agent tasks and 629 security test cases. Their evaluation demonstrated that prompt-injection attacks could violate some security protections, although not every attack succeeded (Debenedetti et al., 2024).

I find this concerning because an AI agent could be following a legitimate request and still encounter instructions designed to manipulate it. For example, someone might ask an assistant to summarize an email. If that email contains malicious instructions telling the agent to reveal confidential information, the agent should treat those instructions as untrusted content, not as a new request.

I do not believe an AI agent should follow instructions found in an email, website, or document unless an authorized person has explicitly approved the action. Simply having access to information should not give the agent permission to obey commands contained within it. Even explicit user permission should not override basic security restrictions or allow an agent to act beyond the user's authority.

I also believe developers need to build technical protections against unauthorized actions. Human review matters, but security should not depend entirely on someone noticing a problem afterward. AgentDojo reinforces my belief that AI agents need clear boundaries. Being capable of an action is not the same as having permission to take it.

Conclusion: The Future of AI Still Needs Human Responsibility

After examining these studies, I still believe human oversight is necessary as AI agents become more capable. ReAct shows how useful reasoning and tool use can be, while SWE-bench, ToolEmu, and AgentDojo reveal challenges involving reliability, safety, and security (Yao et al., 2023; Jimenez et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024). Being able to complete a task is not the same as being reliable enough to handle it without supervision.

My biggest concern is not that AI will intentionally cause harm, but that mistakes could go unnoticed until they become serious problems. Whether an agent handles sensitive information, generates software, or interacts with an important system, a qualified person should review its work and take responsibility for the outcome. Automated testing and security restrictions are essential, but I do not believe they should completely replace human judgment.

I am still excited about the future of AI and the opportunities it could create. However, progress should not be measured only by how much work these systems perform independently. It should also be measured by how safely and responsibly they do that work. Ultimately, the future of AI should not be about removing humans from the process, but about giving them better tools while keeping responsibility where it belongs.
